Boundary curves are mathematical approximations designed to illustrate the pharmacokinetic principles underlying extended-interval aminoglycoside dosing. They are not reproductions of the published Hartford nomogram, which was developed and validated empirically. This educational model assumes one-compartment pharmacokinetics, Vd = 0.26 L/kg, and single-dose (non-steady-state) kinetics. Use validated institutional nomograms or clinical PK software for patient care. Educational simplification: Cockcroft-Gault is calculated here using actual body weight — clinical practice may use adjusted body weight in obesity depending on institutional protocol.

CLINICAL INTERPRETATION

Teaching points

01

Extended-interval dosing exploits aminoglycosides' concentration-dependent killing and post-antibiotic effect — a high peak drives concentration-dependent bacterial killing, and the drug keeps suppressing regrowth even after concentrations fall, so a long drug-free interval doesn't mean lost efficacy.

02

The same drug-free interval that supports efficacy also protects against nephrotoxicity, since aminoglycoside uptake into renal tubular cells is saturable — a sustained trough (rather than a high peak) is what drives toxicity risk.

03

A random level only classifies reliably within the validated 6–14h sampling window — outside that window, the boundary curves aren't meaningful and the level should be redrawn.

04

Extended-interval dosing assumes stable renal function. A rising creatinine between doses is itself clinically informative, independent of any single level.
